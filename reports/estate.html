14 — How it worksWill vs. revocable trust

A Will is public and slow. A Trust is private and fast.

Both are core estate planning documents, and both ultimately distribute to the same beneficiaries — the difference is the route they take to get there.

Will

Testator → probate → beneficiaries

Public recordYes, filed with court
Takes effectAt death, via probate
Speed to beneficiariesMonths to 1+ year
Can be changedAnytime while alive
Not in your plan

No Will appears in the collected set. With the Doe Family Trust holding the bulk of assets, a pour-over Will would govern anything left outside it.

Revocable trust

Grantor → trust → beneficiaries

Public recordNo, stays private
Takes effectImmediately, no probate
Speed to beneficiariesTypically weeks
Can be changedAnytime while alive
In your plan

The Doe Family Trust — $315.9M, 65% of all trust value — carries the residuary estate to the Marital and Credit Shelter Trusts.

Incapacity

Designates a trusted individual to make financial decisions, effective immediately or upon incapacity.

13 — GlossaryKey terms, defined

Key terms, defined.

Assets

Everything you own, including cash, stocks, real estate, collectibles, and cryptocurrency.

Beneficiaries

The people or organizations named to receive your assets.

Grantor

The person who creates a trust and transfers assets into it.

Estate Tax

A tax on the transfer of a person's taxable estate at death, paid by those overseeing the estate.

Taxable Estate

The portion of an estate subject to estate tax after deductions and exemptions.

Exemption

The dollar amount a person can transfer during life or at death before federal estate or gift tax applies.

GST Tax

The Generation-Skipping Transfer tax, applied to transfers made to beneficiaries two or more generations below the transferor.

Irrevocable Trust

A trust that generally cannot be modified or revoked once established, often used to remove assets from a taxable estate.

Probate

The court process of reviewing a Will and authorizing an executor to distribute assets; typically public and fee-bearing.

Advance Health Care Directive

Empowers a named person to make medical decisions if you become incapacitated.

Financial Power of Attorney

Gives a person you choose the ability to conduct financial transactions on your behalf.

Agent / Attorney-in-Fact

The person named in a Financial POA or Healthcare Directive to act on your behalf if incapacitated.
